# Gate Theory

## Working definition

A gate is a control point that admits, modifies, blocks, or escalates a state transition in a reasoning system.

Gates may be human-operated, AI-operated, automated, or distributed across participants.

## Candidate gates

- **Observation Gate:** identifies the permitted evidentiary record.
- **Inference Gate:** checks whether a conclusion follows from presented evidence.
- **Assumption Gate:** exposes dependencies or unsupported premises.
- **Evidence Gate:** evaluates and records support quality.
- **Confidence Gate:** compares confidence to evidence strength.
- **Correction Gate:** detects and routes repairs.
- **Escalation Gate:** routes unresolved or high-impact decisions to appropriate review.

## Gate states

Candidate states:

- **Open:** transition may proceed.
- **Closed:** transition is blocked.
- **Deferred:** transition requires new information or review.
- **Escalated:** transition moves to a higher-authority process.
- **Overridden:** an authorized actor permits a blocked transition.
- **Failed:** the gate does not execute or evaluate as intended.

## Gate failure

Gate failure is not equivalent to bad reasoning. It includes:

- incorrect gate activation
- missed or absent evidence
- incorrect state transition
- stale data
- unauthorized override
- silent failure
- feedback loop without termination

## Gate override

An override is an intentional departure from gate-enforced control. Overrides require:

- identity or authority of the overrider
- reason
- affected state
- new evidence or policy
- risk acknowledgment
- audit trail

## Empirical status

Gate concepts are not yet operational outcomes. Before a gate can be studied, it needs a trigger, input, state transition, observable output, and failure criterion.
